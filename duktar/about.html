Dukhtar, Dispossessed — Speculative Counter-Archive
← Return to Archive

Dukhtar, Dispossessed is a speculative digital counter-archive engaging colonial, imperial, state, and personal image-making around Central Asia. Its holdings draw from Russian imperial survey photography, Soviet state documentation, and post-Soviet nationalist archival projects---formations that have historically constructed Central Asian subjects as beauracratically legible, consumable, and knowable to a western or Russian gaze. The archive also holds a particualr colleciton of documents: adoption records, medical assessments, vaccination cards, state decrees. These are mine.

I was adopted from Russia in 2003, having been born to a TAdjik mother, whom until recently, I knew nothing about other than her country of origin. In reaccessing my brith records, I now have a name and the Russian's government assessment of her character. I am as divorced from "my culture" as one can be---a position I understand as conceptually centrally loadbearing. The archive I inherited is incomplete and unsatisfying as is. This is not unusual. It is the condition of most archives concerning the colonized, the displaced, and the dispossessed.

The construction of an archive is a construction of a history and, by extension, a bet on a future. Reading along the archival grain (Stoler) reveals not only what the archive contains but what anxieties and aspirations produced it. The Turkestan Album, the Uzbek state heritage series, the Open Central Asian Photo Archives: each projects a specific future, for a specific audience, through specific silences. Dukhtar, Dispossessed attempts to produce a different kind of future from the same material, one that does not resolve its incompleteness but foregrounds it.

This archive deteriorates with use. Every cursor movement, every act of access, accumulates damage to the images, to the text, to the structure of the interface itself. This is not strictly metaphorical. Digital objects degrade through constant reproduction and circulation. The act of accessing an archive changes it. What this project argues is that this is not so different from analog decay: a document handled enough times becomes illegible at the fold. The archivist's dilemma--- preservation requires access, but access causes deterioration--- applies equally to digital holdings. But throughout this processing of rewriting, redistributing, reproducing, is something new created? Is a new knowledge produced? Is a new real realized?

And yet: deterioration is also a form of accumulation. The damage map that drives this archive's decay is collective; it is built from the traces of everyone who has read here, simultaneously or across time. Destruction and presence are the same gesture. The archive holds what it is given, and what it is given is the fact of being read. What was not given is not absent. It is elsewhere, or it is gone. This distinction matters and cannot be resolved from here.

Dukhtar is the Tajik and Persian word for daughter. This archive is named for the subjects it holds and for the one subject whose documents it also holds, named and present and still searching. A digital after/alterlife is created, circulated, and coalesced. It's nature is directly dependent on the presence, gaze, and virtual touch of you, the visitor.
