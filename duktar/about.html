Dukhtar, Dispossessed — Speculative Counter-Archive
← Return to Archive

The web, like the archive, is not a neutral container. It has its own institutional grammar (navigation structures, metadata taxonomies, access hierarchies, loading states, URLs as addresses) which carries implicit epistemological claims about what knowledge is, who it belongs to, and how it should be organized. The digital archive as an innovation may be construed as a godsend to many anthropologists and scholars in need of access to otherwise inaccessible collections. It is a deceptively opaque medium and functions essentially in the black box format all institutional archives have in the past. The grain is either to be felt under close scrutiny or intuited via survey. Sometimes it is initial and sometimes it is not; oftentimes it succumbs to the ontological caveats and hegemonic pressure of the internet both as a medium and globalized tool. Dukhtar, Dispossessed proposes that the web archive can be not only a critical medium but an intentionally, collectively, and aspirationally speculative one.

The construction of an archive is a construction of a history. Through both careful and negligent productions of silences, the archive functions as an innately anticipatory project, a projection. By curating a narrative of the past through its treatment and inclusion of documents, the archive imagines a desired or dreaded future. It is an instrument that recursively produces a future by producing a past.

By reading along the grain of three Central Asian archival formations—imperial, nationalist, and diasporic—we can decode each archive as a unique collection of vectors aimed at a specific future, through specific silences, for a specific audience. The archive, and reading along the grain of the archive, becomes a speculative and creative act.

Beyond reading, it also becomes imperative to establish a practice that engages with this creative act, producing and interpolating our own archives outside of the imperium, the state, the homogeny and hegemony that sequesters outliers to the catacombs of catalogs and dust of manila folders. In the post Information Age and the world of digital simulacrum, can collective memory be accessed intentionally and dutifully to create archives that both better represent humanity? Can we seek a radically optimistic, or in Arjun Appadurai's words, aspirational future within the ethics of possibility, through the homegrown and communal archive format?

My own personal history saw me pass through, like many other adoptees, various bureaucratic institutional systems and through a global economy of human import/export. I was born in St. Petersburg Russia to a Tajik mother and an unknown father, relinquished to a Russian orphanage/adoption facility, and eventually adopted at age three by a white American mother and father. With no one in my immediate new environment who could speak Russian, I lost my first tongue to English and never formed the syllables of the Persian Tajik. All the artifacts I retain from before my adoption into America are Russian, not Central Asian, much less Tajik. The only institutionally recognized evidence of my origins are clinically recorded medical documentation and bureaucratic approval of my passage from Russia to America. Of these documents, the vast majority are extensive vaccination records.

While not entirely comparable, the parallels between my own documentation and the state of the current Central Asian state and vestigial imperial archives served as a fertile ground to conceptualize Dukhtar, Dispossessed.

At the core of this web project is the idea that documents as informational, digital assets, still undergo the process of reproduction that physical documents do, and that this process of copying is both irreparably one of deterioration but also consequent of collective engagement. The irony of the notion that reproduction can be degradative is not lost.

The images on this website-as-archive were once printed, inscribed, or chemically captured; after this they were scanned, re-photographed, or manually reproduced a number of times before being uploaded either as .jpg downloads or screenshots from my personal laptop into their current digital housing. Each stage of this process has degraded them in some capacity (imagine a photograph scanned, then its copy scanned, and that copy scanned, and so on). It has, however, also imbued them with new bits of information into the very grain of their "origin." The photocopy bears the smudges and specks of the scanner; the screenshot contains metadata about the precise moment of capture. What is produced is not the original, but an original in its own right, new reality, and even, if we read along the projection of its grain, a new future. Each copy is a new possibility.

The copy can only be produced by the presence of an actor, or frequently, actors. The copy is usually made with the express purpose of distributing the contents of the document along to another without "sacrificing" the integrity or preserved condition of the original. Information is lost and added in varying terms along the way, through both forger and reader. A multi-way exchange occurs between the original author and document, the commissioner and forger, and the reader(s). Something wholly original seeps from these interactions and perpetuates itself along the lines of dialogue, distribution, and digestion.

One must also consider: how original is the original document? Can we really claim the original document is the origin? It is certainly not the inception and not the stimulus; it must also be created. The origin therefore lies elsewhere. The document, the record, the image is even in its original state, a copy of something else. It creates a particular means of looking or reading of the origin (especially as a legible article) and therefore a particular reality or alterlife. And once the origin is either forgotten, these simulacra have alter/afterlives that perpetuate their own histories, and conversely, futures. My childhood visa and my Russian birth certificate and my extensive vaccination records, even in conversation with one another produce an imitation of me; a copy and a new me that now exists as a document legible to the institutional bodies that access it. Those documents propose, or at the very least suggest, a future that the state can understand and satisfy their epistemological anxieties. A new, categorized copy of me now exists, and competes for primacy. I don't feel they are me—but I possess little or no control over their existence. No doubt more copies have and will populate the state repositories of surveillance, be it material or digital. With each copy, is my own agency progressively stripped away?

The online and the digital are ripe landscapes for the exploration and observation of this in action. The sheer magnitude and ubiquity demands a means of copying and thus communication can be near instantaneous and, ideally, accurate. Efficiency often trumps the latter in the hunger borne of consumer-capitalist convenience. The notion of the copy suffers the same treatment as the photograph as a respected marker of "truth" or reality. So when manipulation of the document occurs, the copy is given the benefit of the doubt and swallowed, then regurgitated whole.

For the sake of speed, images are compressed and lose information deemed algorithmically unnecessary. Like the repeatedly scanned image, these .jpgs subjected to tens to thousands of rounds of compression lose data and discernibility; after the end of the process something new is created entirely that is not necessarily understood as a copy of the original but a product of digital metabolism and abstraction. The transmogrified image is less an image of the original, but an image of the process that produced it. And still, it is passed along to the next user.

How then, do we reckon with the digital archive? It necessitates at least some use of digital reproduction for it to be populated. And for it to fulfill its purpose, must it not also be accessed and acted upon, and then further copied by researchers, bureaucrats, hobbyists, and the accidental digital trespasser? However gradual, the accumulated collective presence is the very thing that will eventually degrade the information being housed, no matter how committed to accessibility the archive's archons are. No matter how faithful I am in copying an image from the OCAPA, something is being lost and added in that exchange. The image itself as a copy now serves a new purpose and is in no sense identical to the previous copy, much less the origin. What becomes of that alter/afterlife of the subject? Does the copy continue to violate or does it hide within its process a means of release or possibility via progressive transmogrification?

This inevitable process of degradation is one of loss but that is not to say its exploration and harnessing is without merit. What happens when we critically and intentionally perpetuate this process? What do the new documents become? What new reality, what new future, do they point to?

When we continually degrade the imperial image, uploaded to the Library of Congress as part of its Central Asian imperial survey collection, what becomes of its subject? Is their alter/afterlife erased or released? The reality of the subject is perhaps not fully erased or partitioned from view, but transmogrified into a new conception and grounds for possibility, for an aspiration previously not viable.

Dukhtar, Dispossessed operationalizes the copy logic described above as both subject and method—the website is itself a document that degrades through the accumulation of copies of interaction. Dukhtar is an HTML/CSS based webpage that utilizes javascript, python, and server-side websockets to create a semi-collaborative environment that records and displays up-to-date second-to-second user interactions across multiple devices. Mouse interactions will displace and gradually distort and degrade text, images, and hyperlinks of archival materials sourced from personal archives, imperial, state, and contemporary collections. As gradual degradation occurs, new material and visions appear within the distortions from collective engagement, encouraging the revelation of previously obscured logs and digital materials. Timelines merge and diverge to form a speculation and aspirational futural cultural inheritance.

These mechanics serve as the formal argument of the project, driven by the logics that form the grammar of the archive at large. The archive cannot be returned to a prior state. There is no undo. This digital archive is by no means a functional project. Its effective unusability driven by usage is archival logic at work and applied at scale. At risk of sounding accelerationist, the end state is one of proposed entropy, data reduced to noise.

Dukhtar is the Tajik and Persian word for daughter. This archive is named for the subjects it holds and for the one subject whose documents it also holds, named and present and still searching. A digital after/alterlife is created, circulated, and coalesced. Its nature is directly dependent on the presence, gaze, and virtual touch of you, the visitor.
